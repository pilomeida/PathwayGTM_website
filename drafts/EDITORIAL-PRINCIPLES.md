# Beyond the Brief — Editorial Principles
Compiled 2026-07-03 from every piece of drafting guidance and feedback Pedro has given across the editorial-calendar brainstorm (`~/Desktop/Claude_Corner/brainstorms/2026-07-02-editorial-calendar-design.md`) and the Article 1 drafting rounds. Strengthened the same day after Article 1 took 6 revisions to get right, then strengthened again after a second round of concrete feedback on the same article. The goal from here is one draft that's already correct, with the verification agent confirming rather than discovering problems.

Complementary to `VOICE-NOTES.md`, which covers tone and register. This file covers *what gets included and how it's structured* — content and editorial judgment, not sentence-level style, except where a style rule is forceful enough to restate here (§6).

**This is a gate, not advice.** Every principle below is something a draft either satisfies or doesn't — not a direction to lean toward. If a principle and a nice turn of phrase conflict, the principle wins.

---

## 0. How to use this file (read this first, every time)

1. **Draft against the full checklist from the start.** Do not draft once and then patch principle-by-principle in response to feedback. Sequential single-issue fixes are exactly what produced 6 revisions on Article 1 — each fix satisfied the complaint just raised while quietly breaking a principle that was already satisfied. Write the first draft already trying to pass every section below.
2. **Before presenting any draft, run §9 (Definition of Done) as a literal checklist**, not a vibe check. Tick every line against the actual current text, not against what you intended to do or what an earlier version did.
3. **When a specific fix is requested, re-check every other principle against the fix**, not just the one being addressed. A fix for one principle can silently violate a different, already-settled one (this happened twice on Article 1 — see §10). A fix is not done when the named complaint is resolved; it's done when nothing else broke.
4. **High-leverage surfaces get checked first, not last** — see §9.1. They carry more rhetorical weight per word than body paragraphs and are the easiest place to accidentally reintroduce a problem you already fixed in the body.
5. **The verification agent is a confirmation step, not a discovery step.** If it finds something, that's a signal the self-audit in step 2 wasn't run rigorously enough — not just "one more thing to fix this time."

## 1. Intellectual honesty over a tidy single story

Never reduce a multi-causal problem to one explanation, even when that explanation is well-grounded, well-written, and compelling. If a piece is diagnosing "why X goes wrong," and X has several real, distinct causes, name them all — don't let the best-evidenced or most narratively satisfying one crowd out the others.

**Corollary — no privileged cause, checked everywhere, not just in the paragraphs that name the causes.** When multiple causes are named, give them comparable weight — and "weight" means more than paragraph length. It includes: which one opens the piece, which one gets the title or meta description, which one gets a vivid or foreign-language term, which one gets cited to an external book/source, which one gets the closing line or the final callback, which one gets referenced in a blockquote. A cause can have a perfectly proportionate paragraph and still be privileged through any one of those other channels. Audit each channel separately (see §9.1). The exception is legitimate, not favoritism: a cause that already has, or is scheduled to have, its own dedicated article elsewhere gets a pointer-length mention here, not a full re-explanation (see §8 for exactly how that pointer should read). State that reasoning explicitly *to yourself while drafting* — never to the reader (see §2) — so it reads as a considered structural decision, not an oversight.

**This generalizes beyond "causes" to any list, anywhere.** Whenever an article presents a list — causes, checklist items, examples, options — every item needs comparable substance to every other item. Not identical length necessarily, but comparable enough that no item reads as an afterthought next to another. Apply the same audit to any list before calling a draft done, not just to a causes-of-failure list specifically.

**Never invent evidence.** Real case studies and real STARs only. If a stat or outcome isn't confirmed, park it or flag it — don't publish it to make a point land harder. Cite unread or not-yet-ingested sources at the general-concept level only; never attribute a specific framework, methodology, or statistic to a source that hasn't actually been read. If you paraphrase a source's argument, say "their read is" or "they argue," not "their research shows" or "names precisely" — the second phrasing claims a rigor you haven't verified.

## 2. First-principles structure, not accumulated patches

Before adding a section or paragraph in response to feedback, ask: does this piece's actual *purpose* need this, or am I just addressing the last complaint by patching on top of the previous patch?

**Never let the editorial process leak into the copy.** No references to "a previous version," no language defending the text against a draft the reader never saw. **This also covers explaining a structural choice to the reader** — "so it gets a pointer here rather than a repeat," "a pointer, not a rehash, here" are the same failure mode in a subtler place: the reader doesn't need to know *why* the piece points elsewhere instead of re-explaining something, they just need the pointer itself (see §8). Make the editorial decision; don't narrate having made it.

**Don't build scaffolding just because a topic could be expanded.** A separate section that only exists to summarize or cross-reference the rest of the piece is usually the wrong shape for that information — fold it into the place a reader actually needs it instead (see §4).

## 3. When broadening a piece, don't dilute what was already working

A "make this more complete / more honest" edit should be additive. Don't let it become a wholesale rebalancing that quietly shrinks a good idea to make room for the new material. After any revision, explicitly diff against the prior version for what *disappeared*, not just what got added — a term, a footnote, a piece of texture that mattered can silently drop out while chasing a different fix.

## 4. Elaboration goes where the reader needs it — and never requires the reader to look anything up

If something needs explaining, put the explanation at the point of use. A checklist item's rationale belongs inside that item, not in a standalone "here's why each item matters" section positioned elsewhere. A cross-reference between a cause and where it surfaces in a tool belongs inside the tool, not in a preamble mapping table. If a checklist or list has near-duplicate items, that's a defect — each item must be distinguishable from every other one on a single fast read, not just on close inspection.

**Never make the reader hold a numbered index in their head.** A sentence like "this costs you on checkpoint 2, makes checkpoint 3 inconsistent, and undermines checkpoint 9" forces the reader to flip back and look up what those numbers were — breaking their reading to do bookkeeping the writer should have done instead. Refer to ideas by what they *are* ("an unclear ICP," "consistent messaging," "lead qualification"), never by a number or list position, when connecting them across a sentence or section.

## 5. Grounding and evidence discipline

- Prefer real STARs (career wiki) and real external sources (product-gtm wiki) over anything invented.
- When two sources disagree on a fact (e.g. conflicting figures), flag the discrepancy explicitly rather than silently picking one — resolve only when the source of truth (usually Pedro) confirms.
- STARs should hold success and cost as co-equal truths. Don't let a good process story imply a costless outcome if the real outcome had real costs.

## 6. Voice

See `VOICE-NOTES.md` for the full register (sparing bold, aphoristic blockquotes, bold-lead-in lists, the established section arc). Three rules forceful enough to restate here because they're easy to violate without noticing:

- **Always "we," never "I."** No exceptions, including inside real personal-experience material. See §7 for why.
- **No em dashes.** Plain hyphens, or restructure into two sentences.
- **No company, employer, or product names, ever.** See §7.
- **Category = the article's primary N² Module, not a generic label.** See `VOICE-NOTES.md`'s "Category & format system" section for the 4 categories, their slugs/icons, and the meta-bar's category/format/read-time structure. Set this correctly when drafting — don't leave it as a placeholder to fix later, that's exactly how the site drifted out of sync the first time.

## 7. Anonymization (hard rule — privacy, not style)

Never name a company, employer, product, or "sales object" in article body copy — anything specific enough that a reader could cross-reference it against Pedro's LinkedIn profile and identify the real situation or the real people involved. This applies equally to Pedro's own career history and to Pathway client engagements.

- Characterize generically instead: sector, rough size, the specific mechanics of what happened. The specificity of the situation carries the credibility, not the brand name.
- This is *why* §6's "always we, never I" rule exists — "I" ties a story to one named, publicly identifiable person's timeline. Paired with any specific detail, that's a re-identification path even if the company itself is never named.
- **The private career wiki (`~/Desktop/Claude_Corner/Career/wiki/stars/`) is exempt** — those pages exist for Pedro's own interview use and are never published, so they keep full identifying detail (real company names, real products). The anonymization rule applies at the point of adapting that material into public blog copy, not to the source STAR page.
- Self-check before presenting a draft: could someone who knows Pedro's work history match this passage to a specific employer or product? If yes, generalize further.

## 8. Forward and backward references between articles

**To an already-published article:** just invite the reader, with the link, and nothing else. No explanation of why you're linking instead of repeating the content. ("For more on this, see [Article Title](link)." Full stop.)

**To a not-yet-published article in this series:** don't leave the reader with a bare promise and no substance ("this gets its own treatment later"). Give a one-clause concrete preview of the actual idea, right there, so the sentence carries meaning on its own even before the link exists. Then log the forward reference in `drafts/CROSS-LINKS.md` so the real link gets added retroactively once that article publishes.

**Mechanism — `drafts/CROSS-LINKS.md`:** every time an article contains a forward reference to a not-yet-published piece, add a row to that tracker (source article, the anticipated topic, roughly where the reference sits). Every time a new article is finalized for publishing, check the tracker for rows pointing at it, go back and turn those placeholder mentions into real links in the earlier, already-published articles, and mark the row resolved. This is a required step in §9's Definition of Done for any article that resolves a pending forward reference, not an optional cleanup pass.

## 9. Definition of Done — run this before presenting any draft

Go line by line. Every line must be checked against the *current* text, not against intent.

### 9.1 High-leverage surfaces (audit these first — they carry the most weight per word and are where privilege hides after the body text has been fixed)
- [ ] Title — does it name or imply one cause/idea over others where the piece claims to treat them equally?
- [ ] SEO title and meta description — read in complete isolation from the body. Do they make any claim the body text itself has been corrected to avoid?
- [ ] Opening 2–3 sentences — read in isolation. What idea do they lead with, and is that lead earned or just a drafting habit?
- [ ] Closing 2–3 sentences / final callback — read in isolation. Does it circle back to one specific cause, example, or idea that the rest of the piece treats as one-among-several?
- [ ] Every blockquote — does it name or imply one cause over the others?
- [ ] Any footnote, italicized term, or foreign-language phrase — is it deployed evenly or only for one idea?

### 9.2 Structural checks
- [ ] No sentence anywhere references "a previous version," "we've been guilty of," or otherwise defends the text against a draft the reader never saw.
- [ ] No sentence explains an editorial/structural choice to the reader (§2).
- [ ] No section exists solely to index, summarize, or map the rest of the piece.
- [ ] Every list/checklist item is distinguishable from every other item on one fast read.
- [ ] Every citation to an external source is phrased as attributed opinion/argument unless actually verified.
- [ ] Every STAR or case reference includes real costs/limits where they exist, not just the win.
- [ ] No sentence forces the reader to look up a numbered list item to understand a later reference (§4).

### 9.3 Mechanical / voice
- [ ] Zero instances of "I" in body copy.
- [ ] Zero em dashes.
- [ ] Zero company, employer, or product names — self-check per §7.

### 9.4 Cross-link check
- [ ] Any forward reference to a not-yet-published article logged in `drafts/CROSS-LINKS.md`.
- [ ] If this article's publication resolves a pending row in `CROSS-LINKS.md`, the earlier article(s) have been updated with the real link and the row marked resolved.

### 9.5 Diff check (only applies when revising an existing draft)
- [ ] Compare against the immediately prior version. List anything that disappeared. For each disappearance, confirm it was meant to go, not collateral damage from an unrelated fix.

### 9.6 Self-certification
Before presenting the draft, write one line per section above (9.1–9.5) stating pass/fail and why — in the draft file's own review-notes section, not just in your head. If any line is a "fail," fix it before presenting, not after the verification agent finds it.
